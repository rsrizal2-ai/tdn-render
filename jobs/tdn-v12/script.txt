One year ago today, a fire raced up a Hong Kong tower at ten metres a second. One hundred and sixty-eight people never made it out alive. This week, the final report arrived: one thousand, one hundred and fifty-four pages. And its verdict is brutal. The deadliest fire in Hong Kong since nineteen forty-eight was entirely preventable. Investigators say it began with a single lit cigarette. Then the flames hit the renovation wrapped around the towers: foam boards, and cheap scaffolding nets that were not fireproof, swapped in to save money after a typhoon tore the originals. No alarm ever rang. The power to the fire alarms in all eight buildings had been switched off. Residents filed nearly one hundred and forty complaints. They were largely ignored. And the contract that paid for it all? Three hundred and thirty-six million Hong Kong dollars — the most expensive bid of fifty-seven. Anti-corruption investigators allege bid-rigging and fraud. The city's housing chief resigned the day the report came out. Today, families returned to Wang Fuk Court with flowers, still waiting for justice.
