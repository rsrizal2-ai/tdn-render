On Monday, a former president walks into a courtroom in The Hague — not to give a speech, but to stand trial. Rodrigo Duterte, eighty-one, once ruled the Philippines. Since March twenty twenty-five, he has sat in a detention cell in The Hague, waiting for this week. Prosecutors at the International Criminal Court have charged him with three counts of crimes against humanity: murder, and attempted murder. They allege a widespread, systematic attack on civilians during his war on drugs, between twenty eleven and twenty nineteen. The counts reach back to his years as mayor of Davao City, then to his presidency: alleged murders of so-called high-value targets, and killings in village clearance operations. In all, the charges name seventy-eight victims. His lawyers fire back: this is not a crimes against humanity case, they say, and it does not belong in The Hague. Duterte is presumed innocent. On Monday, the judges will ask him to enter his plea. Then prosecutors open their case: the first of about one hundred witnesses. More than five hundred victims are taking part, and the whole world can watch, streamed with a thirty-minute delay. If convicted, he faces up to thirty years — in extreme cases, life.
