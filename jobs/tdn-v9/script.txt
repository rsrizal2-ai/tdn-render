That bottle of premium olive oil in your kitchen might be the biggest lie in the supermarket. This week, police on five continents unveiled the haul from Operation OPSON Fifteen: a six-month crackdown on fake food, run by Interpol and Europol across fifty-seven countries. Officers carried out more than seventeen thousand inspections, and seized over half a million counterfeit items, more than sixteen hundred tonnes of dodgy food, and almost two hundred and twenty-eight thousand litres of doctored drinks: eighty-seven million dollars of fraud in total. The tricks were brazen. Cheap oil dressed up as extra virgin. Expired food repackaged with fresh dates. Horse meat moved through the chain in secret. Sugar and honey faked and stretched. In Brazil alone, ninety tonnes of adulterated sugar and soybeans were seized. By the end, seventy-nine people were under arrest, and eighteen organised crime groups had been smashed. The draw is simple: supermarket shelves, drug-trade margins, and a fraction of the risk. So read the label twice. The most dangerous thing on your plate might be the story printed on it.
